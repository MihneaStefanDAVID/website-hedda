<p>Change always comes with adaptation.<strong> Should people adapt or should places adapt?</strong></p>
<p>These were the questions we have asked ourselves while analyzing the two references: Kronenhalle Bar by Trix and Robert Hausmann in Zurich and Kleines Caf&eacute; by Hermann Czech in Vienna.</p>
<p>A space is defined not only by its materials, but also by the scale of the human body and the habits it accommodates. At first glance, the Kronenhalle Bar appears unchanged, a place frozen in 1965. Yet it has undergone profound transformations: what was once a gathering place for everyone gradually evolved into an exclusive and luxurious venue. As the value of the paintings on its walls increased, so too did the exclusivity of those permitted, or more so able to afford, to admire them. Originally conceived as a "layover" space for restaurant guests, the bar has become a destination in its own right, drawing visitors from around the world alongside regulars who return for the nostalgic atmosphere of the 1960s. Its luxurious service makes some feel at home, while leaving others with a sense of distance or exclusion.</p>
<p>A mirror to the Kronenhalle Bar can be found in Vienna's Kleines Caf&eacute;, one that sharpens our perspective on the reference point while also opening the way to different interpretations. Both took their initial inspiration from Adolf Loos's American Bar, yet the outcome could hardly be more different. Czech approached the space in an iterative, bottom-up manner: the floor was patched together from mismatched tiles collected across Vienna, and a small extension was later added. The result conveys a sense of improvisation and even chaos, in contrast to the meticulous pre-planning visible at Kronenhalle. The project was guided by ideas such as "transformation as a central concept of architectural theory" and "comfort as an object of architectural theory."</p>
<p>Architecture didn't determine these places, yet the culture and context had their influence. The architecture of each bar sets things in motion, triggering distinct evolutions. Both bars share a domestic scale, but they cater to entirely different social classes.</p>
<p>The first two models were designed this way to create a specific atmosphere achieved through their combination: one feels introverted and enclosed, while the other, with its mirrored surfaces and doubled scale, feels open and expansive. Moreover, the alignment of the entrances forming the "corridor bar" effect highlights that both models stem from the same initial inspiration.</p>
<p>The and combined axonometry focus on the atmosphere each bar creates using only architectural elements: light, materials, colors, views, and the way you move through the space. One feels introverted and enclosed, while the other, with its mirrored surfaces and doubled scale, feels open and expansive. The alignment of the entrances forms the "corridor bar" effect, highlighting that both stem from the same initial inspiration. When you turn the axonometry around, the perspective shifts in the middle, revealing two different ways of seeing the same space. Through the open doors of Kleines Caf&eacute; you immediately feel welcome, but through the tilted mirror you notice a darker, more closed-off space, one you can see but not reach.</p>
