<p>Change always comes with adaptation.<strong> Should people adapt or should places adapt?</strong></p>
<p>These were the questions we asked ourselves while analyzing two references, <strong>two corners</strong>: <em>Kronenhalle Bar</em> by Trix and Robert Haussmann in Zurich and <em>Kleines Caf&eacute;</em> by Hermann Czech in Vienna.</p>
<p>A space is defined not only by its materials, but also by the scale of the human body and the habits it accommodates. At first glance, the <em>Kronenhalle Bar </em>appears unchanged, a place frozen in 1965. Yet what was once a gathering place for everyone gradually evolved into an exclusive and luxurious venue. As the value of the paintings increased, so too did the exclusivity of those permitted, or more so able to afford, to admire them. Once a "layover" space for restaurant guests, the bar is now a destination in its own right, drawing visitors from around the world and regulars seeking the nostalgic atmosphere of the 1960s. Its luxurious service makes some feel at home, while leaving others with a sense of distance or exclusion.</p>
<p>A mirror to the Kronenhalle Bar can be found in Vienna's <em>Kleines Caf&eacute;</em>. Both took their initial inspiration from <em>Adolf Loos's American Bar</em>, yet the outcome could hardly be more different. Czech worked in an iterative, bottom-up manner: the floor was patched together from mismatched tiles collected across Vienna, and a small extension was later added. The result conveys a sense of improvisation and even chaos, in contrast to the meticulous pre-planning of Kronenhalle, guided by "transformation as a central concept of architectural theory" and "comfort as an object of architectural theory."</p>
<p>Architecture didn't determine these places, yet the culture and context had their influence. Both bars share a<strong> domestic scale</strong>, but they cater to entirely <strong>different social classes</strong>.</p>
<p><em>The first two models and axonometries</em> create a specific atmosphere through their unification: one feels introverted and enclosed, while the other, with its mirrored surfaces and doubled scale, feels open and expansive. The alignment of the entrances forms a "corridor bar", showing that both stem from the same initial inspiration.</p>
<p><em>The third model</em>, a "combined corner", is accompanied by an axonometry focusing on the atmosphere each bar creates through architectural elements alone: light, materials, colors, views, and movement. When you turn it around, the perspective shifts in the middle, revealing two different ways of seeing the same space. Through the open doors of Kleines Caf&eacute; you immediately feel welcome, but through the tilted mirror you notice a darker, more closed-off space, one you can see but not reach: Kronenhalle Bar, a place reserved for the upper class or for regular guests who know how to enter and what lies behind the walls.</p>
